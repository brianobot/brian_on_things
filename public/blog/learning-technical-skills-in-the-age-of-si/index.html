Learning Technical Skills in the Age of SI*

What is the motivation and point of even trying when you're constantly faced with the reality that a computer can do it better and fast, why bother learning the basics?

Introduction

Like many technical or skilled persons in the world at the moment it's becoming increasingly hard to feel special about the technical skill you possess, especially a skill like a software mastery or software engineering. I have at some point in recent times come face to face with the reality that what made me special (so to say with the hands in the air defensively) in terms of my technical skills and experience in software programming is largely not special anymore.

Now I have a real issue, Why should I invest real time like was done in the past to gain supreme mastery of topics that now feels very euphmeral, Why should I commit my weekend to understanding how Python's dictionary actually works under the hood, and care about the details linked to open addressing, when I can generate more features far beyond my controls in a product others could just has well vibe code.

At my most recent workplace, apart from the issues linked with lack of proper management and lack of a concrete vision for the company's moat, I basically clocked out at some point, and just became a feature sprouting organism, attending demos after demos to wow always almost-at-the-verge-of-paying-clients. This was sad for me, because outside making a living with the skills I had acquired, I do genuinely love solving problems with the tools in my arsenal.

What was it really about?

This probably justifies most people's stance on whether to bother commiting to learning certain technical skills, if it was solely about implementing a feature, I guess you're better off moving fast and generating one with an LLM model or some agent of sort, but if it was really about mastering systems, understanding principles and unconciously or conciously transferring those skills to other domains then the fear of being replaced should be less (notice how I said less, it doesn't go away). This does not invalidate the fear of losing out on a race of doing so much more with limited time and capital compared to times before now.

For me, it was always about mastery, I mean that mastery made my work easier, but I always loved the bits and bytes of it all, the quirks you had to get use to and understand why there were, the rationales, the processes, it made commiting to mastery very rewarding. All these contributed to buildings ones problem solving muscles, I personally do this thing in my real life activities, where I try to do alot of things concurrently in a beautifully scheduled way, mimicing asynchronouse programming style.

History Repetiting itself

I am sure a certain sect of skilled person experienced things like this in the past, take for example, a painter or an artist, they could have possibly asked themselves, why bother learning to paint and draw when you can get those works done by computers, but still even in the modern world, some of the most priced work of arts are hand made, this doesn't invalidate the craftmanship, and the proliferation of the tools for doing so didn't make everyone an artist.

I think the same argument can be made for what is happening to software engineering and math at this point in history, ofcourse everyone can spin up a React App and get a well documented API powering it, but I believe it's a matter of time, before the hyped dies and people get bored of the AI slop, is there a place of SI in work, ofcourse, but I have accepted that mastery is personal and it does infact reward more even outside of the technical domain of application.

Conclusion

Do whatever makes you happy, and if that includes mastery of the internals of Fortran or memorizing factorials of a very large number, by all means go crazy.
