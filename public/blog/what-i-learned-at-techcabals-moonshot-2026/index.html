What I Learned (and Wish I'd Done Differently) at TechCabal's Moonshot

Lessons from my first TechCabal Moonshot after moving to Lagos: the sessions, the exhibition floor, the people, and three things I'd do differently next time.

Why I Was There

A few months ago, I moved from Port Harcourt to Lagos with one mission: to tap into the city's tech ecosystem by attending events, meeting top talent, and getting the kind of exposure that could change my life.

Then, honestly, not much happened. My first months in Lagos were quiet. No events, no big introductions, just me, a laptop, and a plan that hadn't started yet.

That changed with TechCabal's Moonshot, my first tech event since the move. I loved it, but I also left with a list of things I wish I had done differently. This post covers both.

Arrival

I was so excited that I got to the National Theatre at about 8:30am, an hour and a half before the 10am start. It didn't matter. The place was already swarming with people, and from the energy around me, they were just as excited as I was.

Getting in was painless. I scanned my ticket, collected my tag, and walked in. No queues!

The hard part came after. From the start, I found the central hall confusing to navigate, and I spent my first stretch of the day just trying to work out where everything was.

The Sessions

The sessions are where Moonshot's tech focus really showed. This year's theme was "Courage & Conviction: Building for a New World," and three threads ran through the day for me: AI, the future of money, and building companies that last.

With talks running on several stages at once, I couldn't be everywhere, so I followed the topics closest to what I care about.

  • The State of AI in Nigeria: A panel on where Nigeria actually stands with AI, moderated by Dr. Olubayo Adekanmbi on the Big Tech and Enterprise Stage. It made me see AI as a data and infrastructure problem as much as a model problem. Without good local data, reliable compute, and trained people, the technology stays a demo. As someone with a data engineering background, that was the part of the stack I felt closest to.
  • Stablecoins & Monetary Sovereignty: Seun Lanlege's talk on the Future of Money Stage asked how stablecoins fit into a country's control over its own money. It changed how I think about payment rails. The technology can move value in seconds, but the harder questions are trust, regulation, and who gets to set the rules.
  • Forty Years A Tech Bro: What It Takes To Build Something That Lasts: Sir Demola Aladekomo, in conversation with Fu'ad Lawal, spoke about building across decades instead of funding cycles. It was a useful counterweight to the speed-obsessed side of tech: lasting companies are built on patience and fundamentals more than hype.
  • Building Africa's Workforce for the Global Economy: Joel Ogunsola's talk on the Workshop Stage was about preparing African talent to compete globally. It spoke directly to why I moved to Lagos. Opportunity tends to find people who put themselves in the room, and skills count for more when the right people can see them.

Hearing these ideas in one place made the ecosystem feel far more real than reading about it online ever had.

Walking the Exhibition Floor

If the sessions gave me ideas, the exhibition tour gave me exposure. Every company had a booth with banners, merchandise, and someone ready to explain what made their product different.

My favorite stop was Cloudflare. I'd been wondering whether they plan to support VPS-style machine provisioning for people who deploy on their cloud, so I asked. The answer was no. It wasn't what I'd hoped for, but getting a straight answer from the people who would know was worth the walk.

I also spoke with Liners, a software company that offers reviews and SEO boosting for African software products, and with a sales representative from Rexx, which describes itself as Africa's first AI-native banking solution. Other booths on my route included Grey, a fintech platform for cross-border payments, GoPaddi, an AI-powered platform for travel, tourism, hospitality, and business management, and Zikoko, a Nigerian digital and entertainment platform.

The People

Networking was the reason I moved, so I made a point of talking to people from different corners of tech. These are some of the new friends I made:

  • ID Inyang is a software engineer who builds practical applications for real-world problems. He specializes in backend development and system design, with a focus on software that is scalable, reliable, and efficient. Backend and system design is where I want to go deeper, so he's someone I plan to keep learning from.
  • Brian Ibekwe is a product manager at ReadPulse, where he works on building, improving, and growing the platform. Talking to someone who thinks in terms of users and growth gave me a useful contrast to my own more technical view of products.
  • Ninie: is a mobile videographer/content creator that is passionate about working with brands and individuals to bring their stories to life through engaging visual content
  • Emmanuel Obiabo works mostly with TypeScript and Rust, contributes to open source, and writes about whatever catches his interest. Much of his open-source work has been on blockchain projects in the Stellar ecosystem, and he is a long-time Linux user. You can read more about him here. His path through open source is one I'm curious to follow.
  • Bryan Benibo is a Founding Designer who helps startups build brands and intuitive digital products from scratch, blending creativity with strategy so the design drives real growth and customer engagement.
  • Marilyn is a UI/UX designer. Her portfolio includes TideReader, an AI-powered adaptive reading platform, Delve, a gamified language-learning platform, and Bubblr, a safe social media app for kids. Her case studies are a reminder that products live or die on design, and I'd love to work on something where engineering and design are in the same room.
  • Mauteen is a product design engineer who works where design meets engineering. That overlap is exactly what I want to understand better, so I'm glad we connected.
  • Eric is a creative software developer who builds mobile apps, backend systems, and web platforms end to end. He's a fellow builder, and the kind of person I'd happily build with.
  • Mary Adejumois product manager at Rex Microfinance Bank, where she owns the mobile app and website of the digital bank. She also built FindRyd, an AI-powered navigation app that gives delivery riders directions in landmarks instead of metres, taking it from 85 rider interviews to a tested prototype. A member of the Best Product Discovery Team in the 2026 PLA APM Fellowship, she writes things down until a team can build from them and tests the riskiest assumption first, which is a habit I want to pick up.

What I Wish I Had Done Differently

I'd call these small preparations, not big mistakes. But each one would have made the day easier.

1. Carry a personal card

Over and over, I did the same thing: pulled out my phone, found someone's LinkedIn or X handle, and typed mine in return. It was slow, it interrupted the conversation, and it only worked if both of us had the patience for it.

A personal card would have turned that into a two-second exchange. It doesn't have to be fancy: my name, what I do, and a QR code to my LinkedIn or GitHub. It would also give people something to remember me by when they leave a crowded venue with a bag of merchandise and a head full of names.

2. Wear something that starts conversations

Moonshot was a sea of similar tags and similar outfits, and it was hard to stand out. Branded wear, like a shirt or hoodie with my name, my role, or something I'm building, would have done some of the introducing for me. People would have had a reason to ask about it, and I wouldn't have had to open every conversation cold. It would also have made me easier to find again after a first meeting.

3. Plan my route through the day

I arrived early and still got lost in the halls. With sessions running on several stages at once, it's easy to drift toward whatever is closest instead of what matters. Next time, I'll bookmark my sessions the night before, note which stage each one is on, and leave gaps for the exhibition floor, so I'm choosing my time instead of just reacting to the crowd.

Final Thoughts

I moved to Lagos for moments like this one. Moonshot gave me sessions that sharpened my thinking, a floor full of companies to talk to, and a handful of people I'm glad to know. Next time, I'll show up with a card, an outfit that speaks for me, and a plan for the day. If you were at Moonshot, I'd love to hear what you'd do differently. And if this post was useful, share it with someone heading to their first tech event.
